# Why ambition has to be taught

We have always been held back by what we believe is possible, and we usually trust ourselves only inside a narrow discipline. Michelangelo didn't want to paint the Sistine Chapel ceiling, because he considered himself a sculptor, not a painter.

Psychology calls this **self-efficacy** (Albert Bandura): people who believe in their ability to do something attempt more and achieve more. The useful part is that self-efficacy can be raised on purpose. Each lever below maps onto a move this skill makes, and onto what to say when a user doubts that the top rung is worth trying.

## Lever 1: specific hard goals

Edwin Locke and Gary Latham reviewed roughly 35 years of goal-setting research and found the same thing over and over. People given **specific, difficult goals** outperform people told to "do your best." Vague goals let you stop at the first acceptable answer, and hard specific goals don't.

Patrick Collison keeps a public list called *Fast* of things people built astonishingly quickly when they had an audacious goal and a tight deadline.

**In the skill:** put a number in every rung. Make the depth target look unreasonable.

## Lever 2: believing it's possible

In 1939 George Dantzig, a graduate student at Berkeley, arrived late to a statistics class and copied two problems from the blackboard, assuming they were homework. He turned in solutions a few days later. They were two famous unsolved problems in statistics. Nobody had told him they were impossible.

**In the skill:** tell the agent the hard target is reasonable. When it says "impossible," ask for new approaches instead of accepting the verdict.

## Lever 3: seeing someone else do it

For decades the four-minute mile seemed out of reach. Roger Bannister broke it in May 1954, and John Landy broke it again about six weeks later. Once people had seen it done, the barrier went from physical to psychological, and then it disappeared.

**In the skill:** when a rung looks wild, find the precedent, meaning the team, paper, open-source project or person who already did something like it. An existence proof does more than any pep talk.

## Lever 4: permission to fail

W. Timothy Gallwey's *The Inner Game of Tennis* argues that you play worse when you're trying desperately hard, and better when you let go of the fear of missing. Software has its own version. Linux was announced in 1991 as "just a hobby, won't be big and professional like gnu."

**In the skill:** frame the top rungs as **experiments**: prototypes, shadow runs, one-week spikes. An audacious idea that might fail is easy to say yes to when it runs beside the existing process and costs a week.

## Why this matters more now

Self-efficacy isn't a new problem, but the gap it creates is growing. Model capabilities jump every few months, and they don't grow evenly. They are astonishing in some directions and weak in others. Most people calibrate their ambition once, on whatever the model couldn't do the first time they tried, and never recalibrate. The task this skill exists for is helping people re-ask what's possible, every time.
