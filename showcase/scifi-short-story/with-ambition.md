## Ambition ladder: the dark-side lighthouse, in 5,000 words

**Rung 0 · As asked:** A sci-fi story of 5,000 words or fewer, due in 3 weeks: a lighthouse keeper on a tidally locked planet starts getting messages from the dark side. What you have now: the premise and the deadline. No draft yet, and nothing yet on the contest's rules or what it tends to pick.
**Best axes:** Depth + Range. Depth lets the planet's real physics *cause* the twist instead of just decorating it. Range borrows real lighthouse codes, which already work as a language. I left out Altitude because the story itself is the point, and aiming at "win the contest" instead would flatten it.

### Rung 1 · Stretch — Depth
Keep the premise but ask a better question: not "who's out there?" but "what does she owe them?" The story covers one 11-day orbit, and its clock is the sun bobbing on the horizon. (On an oval orbit the planet wobbles, so the sun rises and sets a little once per orbit.) The senders are the crew she lost on the one dusk she let the light go out.
Judged by: a science-literate reader finds zero physics errors, and every detail traces to a published paper.

### Rung 2 · Bold — Depth + Range   ← recommended
**"Fl R 10s."** The light becomes a wall, and the planet's water cycle becomes the twist. Real climate models of tidally locked planets show wind carrying water vapor to the night side, where it falls as snow and stays. Scientists call this a "cold trap": the coldest place, where water freezes and can't get out. One model even puts liquid water at the edge of the ice. So the colony rations water while the planet's biggest ice sheet sits just past the line its own light draws. Write it with one point of view over one orbit, in five sections keyed to the sun's height. The turn lands near word 3,800 of 4,900.
Needs: the two climate papers, a real light list, 3 cold readers booked for day 9 · Judged by: the midpoint guess test (in the prompt below), then the contest itself.

### Rung 3 · Audacious — Range
**The Slow Sentence:** nine keepers, 270 years, one logbook, about 540 words each. Night-side life is so cold it thinks one word per generation. The first keeper flashed her drowned son's name into the dark. The last keeper, the week the light is automated, receives the final word of the answer. · Judged by: cold readers can follow all nine keepers without headings, and 2 of 3 say the ending moved them, not just impressed them.

**Recommended: Rung 2.** It's the biggest story you can draft by day 7 and still revise twice: a society's founding crime and one sister's choice, both earned by real physics. Le Guin fit a whole society's founding bargain into "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" in about 3,000 words. Try Rung 3 only if you could draft nine voices in week one.
**Unlocks:** contest rules + last 3 years' winners (set up, 1 hour) · Menou 2013, "Water-Trapped Worlds" ([arXiv:1304.6472](https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.6472)), and Leconte et al. 2013, *A&A* 554, A69 (set up, free) · the US Coast Guard Light List, for real flash codes and red sectors (set up, free) · 3 cold readers who haven't heard the premise, plus 1 science reader (set up: ask today)
**Trust risks:** The contest may ban AI-written text, as many now do → you write every sentence; AI only researches, checks the physics and critiques. Check the rules today. · The twist could read like a lecture → the physics only appears as things she does: rationing, scraping frost off the lens, watching the sun.

**What it would look like** (sketch of the whole concept, turn and ending included):
> Mara keeps the Terminator Light. Its red sector has faced the night for sixty years (real lighthouses show red over dangerous water), and in colony code red means *no passage*. Her grandmother lit it the night the founders sent 400 people into the dark to save water. In dry years the colony still sends people out: last orbit it sent her brother Teo. The next lottery is eleven days away.
> Out in the black she sees a light that's on no list: two short flashes, one long. It's the signal she and Teo used with flashlights as kids.
> Midpoint: for sixty years the old logbooks record lights to the north, "disregarded per standing orders." The first entry is in her grandmother's hand: *Exhibited Fl R 10s, 0400. God forgive the council.*
> The turn: the exiles didn't die. They live at the edge of an ice sheet the size of a continent, powered by the wind that never stops blowing off it. They've had the planet's water all along, and they stayed out because the red light told them to. Teo's message isn't *let me come home*. It's *WE CAN BRING WATER. PUT OUT THE RED.*
> Ending: there's no council vote. She climbs into the lantern room and lifts the red glass out of its frame. For the first time since it was lit, the light shows white over the dark. One light on the ice starts moving toward her, then forty.

**Upgraded prompt** (paste this):
```
Goal: a <=5,000-word sci-fi story for [CONTEST], due [DATE], that a judge reading hundreds of entries still remembers a week later.
Concept "Fl R 10s": a lighthouse on the terminator of a tidally locked planet whose red sector has faced the night side for 60 years
as a wall against exiles. Twist: the planet's water is cold-trapped on the night side, and the exiles have it.

Rules: First find [CONTEST]'s AI policy and quote it to me. I write every sentence of the story. You do research, structure,
physics checks and critique only. Do not draft story prose.

Raw inputs (read the sources, not summaries):
- The contest rules and its last 3 years' winning stories: what this judge actually picks
- Menou 2013 "Water-Trapped Worlds" (arXiv:1304.6472); Leconte et al. 2013, A&A 554, A69: nightside ice trap, ice-edge reservoir, surface winds
- Libration of tidally locked planets on eccentric orbits: how far the star bobs at the terminator each orbit
- USCG Light List: light-characteristic notation and red danger sectors

Steps:
1. World bible: 10 physical rules (orbit period, libration amplitude, terminator temperature, wind direction, ice location),
   each cited to a source. Flag anything the plot needs that the physics doesn't support.
2. Beat sheet: 5 sections keyed to the sun's height over one orbit, with word budgets. Brother's signal by word 800,
   logbook discovery ~2,500, water turn ~3,800, ending <=4,950.
3. Plant list: every clue that makes the water turn feel inevitable in hindsight, and where it sits.
4. After my day-7 draft (~6,500 words): reverse-outline it, mark every sentence that explains physics instead of showing it
   as action, and propose cuts to <=4,950 (about 25%).

Hard targets: draft day 7; final <=4,950 words day 18; submit day 19.
Outside judges (not you): 3 cold readers, stopped at word 2,500 and asked "What do the lights want?" (at most 1 of 3 says water);
at the end they mark lines where they skimmed (target: zero in the last 1,000 words). 1 science reader checks the world bible.
Experiment: I write two openings (log entry vs. scene); keep the one where more readers wanted page 2.
```

Say "go" to run it, or pick another rung.
