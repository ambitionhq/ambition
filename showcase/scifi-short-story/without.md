# The most ambitious version you can actually pull off

At 5,000 words, more plot won't make the story more ambitious. What makes it ambitious is how much time and meaning you can fit on one small stage. So here's the version I'd push you toward. It covers 140 years and two civilizations, but nearly everything on the page happens in one tower, to one person, over a few weeks.

## The pitch

A colony clings to the terminator of a tidally locked world, where the sun has been setting for four hundred years. On a headland at the edge of the ice stands a lighthouse that warns ships off the icebergs breaking away from the nightside ice sheet. Every lighthouse has a *character*, the flash pattern sailors use to identify it. This one's is three flashes every fifteen seconds, written Fl(3) 15s. It has never changed. Keepers hand down one creed: **never change the light.**

This season the ice shelf is breaking up, and the icebergs are bringing things out of the dark: carved bone, wrapped cloth, writing.

A hundred and forty years ago the colony exiled a group of people into the night. Everyone knows they died in the first winter; kids learn it in school. They didn't die. They've been writing to the lighthouse the whole time, in the only way the dark allows: on the ice, which takes a lifetime to reach the coast. For a century the logbooks have recorded what arrived as *"Debris on north shingle. Cleared."*

The oldest messages are in plain English. The newest are in an English that has drifted for five generations and turned into liturgy. The keeper sorts them by that drift and pieces their history together backward. The last thing she finds is the first letter they ever sent. It restates a deal they made with the first keeper on the day of the exile: *Never change the light, unless they pardon us. Then show two flashes, and we'll come home.*

Her creed is the first half of their sentence. The first keeper made the promise. No pardon ever came, and he handed down only the half he could safely say out loud. Both sides preserved the same sentence. On the dayside it hardened into a rule. In the dark it became a prayer.

Everyone who meant "no" has been dead for a century. Only the light still says it.

She hangs her coat over a panel of the lens. Two flashes.

## Why this is the big version

- **Light out, ice back.** Her signal reaches them at the speed of light, and their words take a lifetime to come back. Every message she reads was written by someone long dead to someone long dead. If the messages came by radio, your premise would be a mystery about *who* is out there. Sent by ice, it becomes a story about *time*. You still get "messages from the dark side," and they become objects she can hold.
- **The reveal is about meaning, not a twist.** Nobody turns out to be a ghost, a clone, or the keeper herself. The click comes when she recognizes her own creed inside their letter. That changes how the reader sees the whole story without tricking them. It will hit harder with judges who have read a hundred twist endings.
- **The antagonist is a value.** Her main virtue is constancy, and it turns out to be the cruelty. You don't need a villain. The Council voice on the radio should be kind and bored.
- **The climax is one gesture.** A coat over glass is a tiny physical act that carries enormous meaning, which is exactly the scale a short story can hold.
- **A long timespan on a small stage.** The story covers 140 years and two societies. On the page, though, there's one tower, one keeper, one radio voice, and about six messages.

## Make the planet do plot work

Every physical fact should either make something happen or be part of her daily life. These ones earn their place:

- **The sun never moves.** From the terminator it sits on the horizon forever. There are no days, so time is kept in watches and by winding the clockwork that turns the lens. Date her log entries by windings.
- **The wind always blows out of the dark.** On a tidally locked world, air near the ground flows from the cold nightside toward the hot dayside. Everything from the night arrives on that wind: cold, snow, smells.
- **The ice has to go somewhere.** Snow falls on the nightside without end, so the ice sheet creeps toward the light and breaks off into the sea. That one fact explains why ships need a lighthouse and how the exiles send their mail.
- **The loom.** This is a real lighthouse term: the glow of a light seen from beyond the horizon, reflected off clouds. Moist dayside air flows into the night and forms clouds, and the beam writes on them. That's how people a hundred kilometers into the dark have seen three flashes every fifteen seconds all their lives. It's the only light in their sky.
- **Why now.** One sentence is enough. A warm year breaks up a century's worth of shelf ice at once and delivers it all jumbled together. That explains both the flood of messages and why they arrive out of order. Don't explain the glaciology on the page.

She knows all of this and would never think it out loud. Let it come through in her chores.

## The structural trick: language drift as a clock

The messages arrive jumbled, and she dates each one by how far its language has drifted. Show them to the reader roughly newest first. That way the reader travels backward through the exiles' history while she moves forward toward her decision. The language gets *clearer* as the story goes on, and the full meaning arrives last.

Here's the idea as a ladder. These lines only illustrate the principle, so invent your own:

| Message | Sample |
|---|---|
| Newest | *Tu-flash, an we cum. Tu-flash, an we cum.* |
| Older | *Thre-flash sez stay. We stay gud.* |
| Older still | *We ar still heer. Thre flash, still thre.* |
| Early | Plain English: births, deaths, the heat vents, who's sick. |
| First letter | The deal, restated in full. |

Some rules:

- Spelling drifts first, grammar second, and meaning last.
- The newest message must still make sense to an attentive reader.
- Keep most messages to one to three lines, and use six at most.

*Riddley Walker* and the "Sloosha's Crossin'" section of *Cloud Atlas* show how far drift can go. Then do much less than either.

The first letter is the only long one, maybe 120 words. Put her creed in it word for word, buried inside an "unless" sentence, so the reader recognizes it rather than being told. Give it one personal line that makes the first keeper human. For example, his daughter went with the exiles, and the letter says she misses him.

## The ending

She changes the light's character without a pardon, on her own authority. She knows three things:

- The Council will switch it back to Fl(3) within hours, as soon as ships report the change.
- Ships navigate by that character, so she's breaking the one rule that keeps people alive.
- In the dark this isn't a reply. It's a prophecy coming true, and she has no idea what that will do to a culture.

Don't show the exiles coming, and don't show how the Council responds. End on official language. My pick is a **Notice to Mariners**, the real document that announces changes to lights: *"Effective immediately, the light at Terminus Head is altered from Fl(3) 15s to Fl(2) 15s."* The whole story has been about official language hiding a human question. Ending on official language that carries the human answer closes the loop. The alternative is her log entry *"Reason for alteration:"* followed by whatever single word she writes.

Spend an afternoon learning how group-flashing Fresnel lenses work, so the coat gesture is mechanically right. That's the one place where technical accuracy really pays off.

Title options: *Fl(3) 15s*, *The Loom*, or *Character*. Use *Character* only if you can resist pointing at the pun.

## Word budget (aim for 4,700–4,850)

| Section | Words |
|---|---|
| Her routine, the world, the first object | ~700 |
| Objects pile up; the "debris cleared" logs | ~1,000 |
| Sorting by drift; the prayer; the bored radio call ("Exiles? They died the first winter.") | ~1,200 |
| The first letter and the creed | ~600 |
| The decision: her mentor's rule, the ships, the cost | ~800 |
| The coat and the notice | ~500 |

## Traps

1. **The planet lecture.** If a paragraph explains physics and nothing happens in it, cut it.
2. **Cute or unreadable drift.** Push it too far and it becomes a puzzle. Make it too cute and it becomes a joke. Test it on someone.
3. **Over-ending.** Any scene after the coat goes on the lens weakens the ending.
4. **A villainous Council.** Making them malicious would shrink the story. Their indifference is the point.
5. **A second plot.** No romance, no colony politics, no chapter set out in the dark, no flashback to the exile. The 140 years should exist only in the messages and the logbook.

## Three weeks

- **Week 1:** Write a one-page sheet of the world's rules, not its history. Next, write the messages and the first letter, because they're the story's skeleton. Then write a fast, rough full draft. Running long is fine.
- **Week 2:** Rework the structure and cut to about 5,200 words. Give it to one reader who knows nothing about it and ask two things: *When did you work out what two flashes meant?* and *What did you feel at the last line?* If they say "page two," make the prayers murkier. If they say "never," make the first letter plainer.
- **Week 3:** Line-edit down to length, read it aloud, and submit two days early. Also check the contest's rules on AI help. Many contests ban AI-generated text, so the prose needs to be entirely yours. The sample lines above are only illustrations.

## Turning ambition up or down

- **Too much?** Drop the first keeper's promise. The prayer and the first letter still carry the story. You lose the matching rule and prayer, but you keep the heart.
- **Want more?** Tell the whole story in documents: log entries, the messages, radio transcripts, the Notice, with no normal narration. This has the highest ceiling and the highest risk, because her inner life has to show in what she chooses to write down and what she stops writing down.
